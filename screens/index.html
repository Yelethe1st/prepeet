Use cases

Six domains, six rubrics, six very different conversations

A nursing escalation question and a systems design question are not the same interview. Each domain has its own competency set, its own follow-up logic and its own anchored rubric, maintained with subject-matter reviewers.

Software engineering

Senior Backend Engineer

Competency in focus: debugging & incident response — does the candidate describe the diagnosis, or only the fix?

“Your booking service is healthy but writes to the patient record are timing out. Walk me through your first ten minutes.”

Product management

Senior Product Manager

Competency in focus: prioritisation — is there a stated trade-off, or just a list of everything that matters?

“You have one engineer for six weeks and three customers each threatening to leave over a different gap. How do you choose, and what do you tell the other two?”

Nursing

Registered Nurse — Intensive Care

Competency in focus: escalation & handover — who gets called, when, and what is said in the first sentence.

“Your ventilated patient’s mean arterial pressure drops to 52 and the registrar is in theatre. Talk me through what you do and who you escalate to.”

Teaching

Secondary Mathematics Teacher

Competency in focus: assessment & feedback — does the plan change because of the data, or in spite of it?

“Two thirds of your Year 9 class got the same simultaneous equations question wrong. What does tomorrow’s lesson look like, and how do you know it worked?”

Sales

Enterprise Account Executive

Competency in focus: commercial qualification — is the deal qualified on paper, or on hope?

“Your champion loves the product, but procurement says the security review adds nine weeks. What do you do this week, and what do you change in the forecast?”

Finance

“Theatre staffing has come in 11% over budget for the third month running. How do you find out why, and what is the one line you put in front of the COO?”

Product

Built for the part everyone skips: the write-up

Running the interview is the easy half. The hard half is producing something a hiring panel can argue with — and a candidate can appeal.

Realtime voice, not a form with a microphone

Sub-second turn-taking over WebRTC. The interviewer hears you pause, waits, and asks the follow-up your answer actually invited. You can interrupt it; it will let you.

Evidence-linked evaluation

Every competency score is assembled from spans of the transcript, each with a timestamp and a playable audio range. Click a score, land on the sentence. Nothing is scored from tone, pace or hesitation.

Calibration and rubric anchors

Each rubric level carries a written anchor and two real example answers. Tenants run a calibration set before go-live and re-run it whenever a rubric or model version changes; drift is reported, not hidden.

Practice coaching that is specific

Practice mode returns per-answer feedback, a rewritten version of your own answer, and the one question most likely to expose the same gap next time. Retry any question as many times as you like.

Recruiter review tools

Side-by-side candidate comparison on the same rubric, coverage and confidence flags, structured reviewer notes, and a decision that is recorded as a person’s decision — with their name on it.

Multi-tenant governance and audit

Per-tenant data residency, retention windows, SSO and SCIM, role-scoped access down to a single requisition, and an append-only audit log covering every view, export and override.

How it works

Four steps — then the part that matters, who sees what

Same interview engine, same rubric, two very different products on either side of it.

  1. 1

    Set the interview up

    A recruiter picks a role, a rubric version, a shape (behavioural, technical, case, panel) and a time box, then sends an invitation. A practice candidate picks their target role and an interviewer persona instead.

  2. 2

    Have the conversation

    15 to 60 minutes of live voice. Microphone check first, captions throughout, push-to-talk if the room is noisy, and a hard time box the candidate can see counting down.

  3. 3

    Extract the evidence

    The transcript is segmented into answers, and each answer is matched to rubric anchors. Spans are tagged as supporting, contradicting, unverified claim, or gap. Thin competencies are marked, not guessed.

  4. 4

    Review and decide

    Practice candidates get coaching and a progression view. Recruiters get an evidence pack and make the decision themselves. Prepeet never advances, rejects or ranks anyone on its own.

Practice mode vs screen mode: exactly who sees what

This boundary is enforced in the product, not by policy. In screen mode, a candidate is never shown a score, a band, a competency chart, coaching, a recommendation or a reviewer note — only confirmation that their interview was submitted.

The live conversation

It behaves like an interviewer, including when you cut it off

Most “AI interviews” are a queue of recorded questions. Prepeet holds one continuous conversation: it tracks what you have already covered, notices when two answers disagree, and asks about it — in the session, while you can still respond.

  • Interruption handling. Start talking and the interviewer stops mid-sentence, keeps your first words, and resumes from where you took over. Barge-in is on by default and can be disabled per tenant.
  • Phase progression. Warm-up, core competencies, depth probes, candidate questions, close. Each phase has its own time budget, visible to the candidate the whole way through.
  • Live captions. Every word the interviewer says is captioned in real time, with an adjustable text size and a full scrollback.
  • Push-to-talk fallback. On a noisy line or a shared room, hold the space bar (or the on-screen button) to talk. Barge-in turns off automatically in this mode.
  • Reconnection. If the network drops, the session pauses, the clock stops, and the candidate has ten minutes to rejoin on the same link. Nothing already said is lost.

Evidence and feedback

A score you can’t trace back to a sentence isn’t a score

Prepeet builds each competency from tagged spans of the candidate’s own words. Open any score and you get the quote, the timestamp and the audio. Where the conversation never produced enough material, the competency reads insufficient evidence — a deliberate blank, not a low number.

  • Traceable by construction. A competency cannot be published without at least three independent supporting spans; below that it is withheld.
  • Contradictions are surfaced, not averaged. Conflicting answers appear as their own evidence type so a reviewer can judge them.
  • Nothing inferred about the person. No personality inference, no emotion or honesty detection, no prediction of how someone will perform in the job.
  • Confidence ranges, always visible. Every score carries the interval it was derived from, so a 66 with a wide range never reads like a 66 with a narrow one.

Questions we get asked first

The awkward ones, answered plainly

If the honest answer is “no, we don’t do that”, it says so.

No. Prepeet produces evidence: a transcript, tagged spans, competency signals with confidence ranges, and coverage flags. It does not advance, reject, rank or shortlist anyone. A named person makes every decision and their name is recorded against it. Tenants can require a second reviewer for any outcome, and screen candidates can request a human re-review of the interview.

No — and we will not build it. Prepeet does not analyse tone, pitch, accent, speaking rate, hesitation, facial expression or “cultural fit”, and it makes no prediction about how someone will perform in the job. It evaluates the content of what a candidate said against a written rubric, and nothing else. If a feature request needs an inference about the person rather than the answer, the answer is no.

The interview itself, a visible timer, live captions, and at the end a confirmation page saying the interview was submitted and roughly when they will hear back. That is all. No score, no band, no competency chart, no coaching, no recommendation and no reviewer note is generated or stored anywhere a screen candidate can reach. Coaching output is not merely hidden in screen mode — it is never produced. Practice mode is the opposite: candidates see everything about themselves.

Each tenant is pinned to a region at creation — EU (Frankfurt) or US (Virginia) — and audio, transcripts and evaluations never leave it. Retention is set per tenant, from 30 days to 24 months; the default for screen interviews is 18 months, after which audio and transcripts are deleted and only the anonymised decision record remains. Candidates can request deletion of a practice session at any time from their settings. Deletion is a real delete, including backups, within 35 days.

Prepeet targets WCAG 2.2 AA throughout. Live captions are always on by default and resizable, the whole interview is keyboard operable, push-to-talk replaces voice activity detection where that is easier, and extra time can be granted per invitation without the candidate having to disclose why. Where a spoken interview is not a reasonable format at all, the recruiter can withdraw the invitation and run the conversation themselves — a request for an adjustment is never recorded as a candidate outcome.

The session pauses and the clock stops. Everything said up to that point is already committed, so nothing is lost. The candidate has ten minutes to rejoin using the same link, and the interviewer picks up with a short recap of where you were. If they cannot get back, the session is marked ended early and the recruiter sees a coverage warning rather than a low score — an interview that was cut short is reported as short, not as weak. Re-issuing the invitation takes one click.

Four ways, none of them a silver bullet. First, scope: only rubric-relevant content is evaluated, and speech characteristics are excluded by design. Second, anchors: every rubric level has written criteria and worked examples, so scoring is comparison against text rather than against an impression. Third, calibration: tenants run a fixed evidence set before go-live and again on every rubric or model change, and the drift report is visible to them, not just to us. Fourth, measurement: adverse-impact reporting by role and rubric version is available to tenant admins, and we publish our known limitations — accented speech recognition accuracy and domain coverage outside our six maintained rubrics are the two we are least happy with today. A human still decides, which is the most important control of all.
